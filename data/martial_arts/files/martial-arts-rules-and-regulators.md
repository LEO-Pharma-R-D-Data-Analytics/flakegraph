# Rules and Regulators in Combat Sports
How federations, commissions, and written standards shape competition

## Governance has a defined scope

The statement that an organization governs an art needs boundaries. Federations usually govern competitions held under their authority; they do not control every independent school or all cultural practice bearing a similar name. The International Judo Federation and the International Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu Federation provide rules for prominent international circuits, while local and alternative organizations continue to exist.

The canonical sentences use language tied to competition for this reason. Reviewers should interpret GOVERNED_BY as a regulatory relation within a stated domain, not as cultural ownership. That reading preserves the usefulness of federation nodes and avoids erasing communities whose practice sits outside a major tournament system. It also makes contrary evidence easier to evaluate at the right scope.

International judo competition is governed by the International Judo Federation under its published sporting rules.

Many prominent Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu tournaments are governed by the International Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu Federation's rules.

## Mixed martial arts and commission coordination

Professional MMA in the United States is regulated through state and tribal authorities rather than one national license. The Association of Boxing Commissions supports coordination among many of those bodies. Its shared rules help officials, promoters, and athletes understand rounds, weight classes, judging criteria, medical checks, and prohibited actions across participating jurisdictions.

The Unified Rules are a living regulatory work with amendments and local adoption decisions. Calling the association an author and maintainer captures collective institutional responsibility without attributing the text to one celebrated individual. ## International federations and Olympic formats

Sport karate and Olympic-style taekwondo depend on detailed competition rules. The World Karate Federation defines areas, scoring, penalties, kata procedures, and event administration for its circuit. World Taekwondo defines electronic scoring, protective equipment, legal target areas, and match procedure for its discipline. These systems represent regulated sport formats rather than every way the arts are taught.

Governance changes technique by changing incentives. A scoring rule can favor speed, control, visible impact, or positional dominance. Protective equipment can permit one kind of contact while discouraging another. A canonical historical corpus should therefore connect arts to governing bodies without treating rules as neutral containers. They are active parts of how modern competition is practiced and perceived.

International sport karate events are governed by the World Karate Federation under its competition rules.

Olympic-style taekwondo competition is governed by World Taekwondo under its published rules.

## Rules in boxing and savate

The Queensberry rules are often used as shorthand for modern boxing's transition, but the change was gradual. Earlier rules, promoters, fighters, and enforcement practices also mattered. John Graham Chambers drafted the named 1867 rules, which gained visibility through the Marquess's patronage. Their historical importance does not make them the sole source of every modern boxing convention.

Savate's current French federation represents a later layer of institutional organization. It governs national competition and supports technical disciplines with specified levels of contact. Placing this relation beside boxing's written rules shows two regulatory forms: a work that influenced a sport's codification and an organization that currently administers a defined field of competition.

A major stage in modern boxing was documented in the Marquess of Queensberry Rules published in 1867.

The Marquess of Queensberry Rules were authored by John Graham Chambers and issued under aristocratic patronage.

National competitive savate in France is governed by the French Savate Federation.
